# Product strategy: the general framework

> **Category:** Product Strategy · **Type:** strategic analysis (threats, competitive response, market entry, monetization, new tech). Also the rubric for every strategy type. · [All rubrics](README.md)

**In one line:** Laying out options is easy. Choosing one and defending it against the best
counter-argument is the hard part, and the hard part is what gets scored. Anchor to how the
company actually makes money and to what changed recently, set principles that eliminate
options, and close with a call that can take a punch.

---

## What they want to hear
- **A point of view.** "Here's what I would do" beats "here are three reasonable options."
- **The real business:** what the company makes money from, not its mission statement.
  Meta talks about connection but runs on ads.
- **A current market view:** what changed, who it affects, and why it matters here. Never just
  "AI is changing everything."
- **Reasons to say no** to the alternatives.
- **A recommendation that can take a punch:** name the best argument against it, then explain
  why you'd still do it.

## The framework (6 steps)

**1. Clarify** with two or three questions about scope and success, not features. Ask about the
time horizon (a 6-month decision and a 3-year strategy are different questions), what success
looks like and who measures it, and constraints like budget, regulation, or existing bets. If the
interviewer leaves it open, state your assumption ("12-month horizon, revenue not engagement")
and move on.

**2. Anchor to business goals.** Come in knowing how the company makes money, what growth looks
like at its stage, and where it's spending. *Mission ≠ business model.* At senior level, name
the tension between them and take a side. For example, Meta's mission argues against flooding
the feed with AI content, while its ad engine seems to favor it. The cautious call wins, because
synthetic content erodes the social signal that ad targeting depends on.

**3. Map the landscape.** Be specific:
- **The threat or opportunity, and who it hits.** AI Overviews hurts publishers who rank for
  informational queries and monetize traffic. Paywalled publishers feel it differently.
- **Competitors' strategic posture, not their features.** Are they going broad or deep,
  defending or attacking? What is their theory of how they win?
- **The timing signal:** a tech unlock, market shift, regulation, or competitor move. If you
  can't answer "why is this the question now?", you're not ready to answer it.

**4. Set guiding principles.** These are constraints that eliminate options. Most candidates
skip this step, which is why their final call feels disconnected from their analysis.
- Take your biggest business observation and ask what it rules out. That's principle 1.
- Take your biggest market observation and ask what kind of response it makes irrelevant or
  harmful. That's principle 2.
- Take the biggest tension (business vs. threat, short vs. long term, user vs. revenue) and make
  it a tiebreaker. That's principle 3.
- **If a principle doesn't rule anything out, it isn't a principle.**

**5. Generate, then filter.** Brainstorm unfiltered for 60 to 90 seconds and write down more
options than you'll use, so you don't anchor on the obvious first one. Then cut everything that
breaks a principle. **More than three on the final shortlist means the filter didn't work.**

**6. Argue and close.**
- Give a specific recommendation, with a time horizon, in two sentences. Not "it depends," and
  not "a combination."
- **Name the strongest alternative and defeat it.** That's the difference between a decision
  and a preference.
- Say what evidence would make you reconsider.

## Staying current: a habit for every strategy and product sense round
1. **Passive intake:** follow 5 to 10 sharp people, and set up a daily briefing tuned to your
   target companies (their news, big-tech moves, AI policy).
2. **Active synthesis, about 10 minutes, three times a week:** take one move and ask what the
   company's position was before, why now, who benefits and who's threatened, what they're trying
   to validate, and what you'd have done differently.
3. **Hands-on, 5 minutes:** try the product. What's good, what's rough, what would you change
   first?

An OpenAI candidate found the interviewer didn't care about the research itself, only what they
would *do* with it. Knowing the landscape is the minimum. Having a view on the next move is what
counts.

## Rubric: all strategy questions (5 equal dimensions, Strong No Hire → Strong Hire)
Acing three and flopping two is a no hire.
1. **Business model fluency.** Strong Hire: surfaces the gap between stated goals and revealed
   behavior and uses it to filter options; knows where the company is in its growth arc. Anchor to
   the revenue model, not the About page. At Stripe, treating it as "a dev-tools company" misses
   the payments cost structure.
2. **Landscape accuracy.** Specificity and timing, plus posture rather than a feature catalog.
   Tell threat models apart: Google entering a space is a distribution threat, while an AI-native
   startup is an architecture threat. Strong Hire: the interviewer learns something.
3. **Decision quality.** The failure is giving no answer. Strong Hire: names the strongest
   counter-argument unprompted, names the signal that would change course, and is specific enough
   to be wrong.
4. **Structural clarity.** Each step builds on the last. Say the connection out loud: "Because X,
   and Y rules out Z, the only option that survives is…"
5. **Genuine POV:** the dimension AI can't fake, and the one that separates candidates now. Test:
   *could five other prepared candidates give this exact answer?* Strong Hire: says something
   unexpected that the interviewer remembers. At Anthropic, treat safety as a real problem, not a
   talking point. At OpenAI, connect to the AGI mission.

## Traps
- Laying out options and never picking one.
- Feature comparison instead of posture.
- "AI is disrupting the space" with no specifics.
- Principles that don't rule anything out.
- A recommendation that sits next to the analysis instead of following from it.
- A shortlist longer than three.
