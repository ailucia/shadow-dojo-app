# Behavioral (Leadership & Drive): the five themes and the story bank

> **Category:** Leadership & Drive · **Type:** "Tell me about a time…" Also level signaling, building and practicing stories, and the rubric for every L&D type. · [All rubrics](README.md)

**In one line:** These questions now make up half or more of the evaluation at top companies,
and they're where your *level* gets decided. Keep about ten stories you know deeply, tagged so
each fits several questions. Memorize *anchors*, not scripts: a strong opening, three must-include
scenes (stakes, the key decision, the biggest challenge), and a close about what changed. Stories
should read as *lived*, at the scope of the level you're targeting.

---

## What they're scoring
- **The right scope for your target level:** a feature, the team's direction, a cross-org effort,
  or a company-wide bet.
- **Named stakeholders, real tension,** and how you changed a specific person's mind.
- **Problems you found and pursued** that nobody assigned.
- **A real story:** stakes, a genuine decision with a real alternative, and a close about change,
  not just a metric.
- **Lived, not recited.** Passion is now the biggest differentiator, because AI-assisted prep has
  made polish common.

**Senior bar:** the scope is visible (who was involved, what changed at org or company level, why
it mattered beyond the project); a *decision* sits at the center, not a list of activities; it
closes on a transferable insight; and the problem was one *you* identified.

## Level signaling (this round measures level more than skill)
- A weak behavioral round leads to a **downlevel or a rejection.** A downlevel isn't a reliable
  fallback (the team needs room at both levels). OpenAI reportedly downlevels by default. Better to
  earn the level and choose to step down yourself.
- **Interviewers pattern-match** against candidates they've seen at each level, on five things:
  - **Scope of the problem you owned** (the biggest signal).
  - **Level of ownership:** did you define the problem, or were you handed a task?
  - **Stakeholder management:** *which* stakeholders, what they resisted, how you moved them. "I
    aligned stakeholders" means nothing.
  - **Gravitas, poise, passion:** delivery outweighs words.
  - **Strategy-to-execution ratio:** it should match the target level.
- **What each level sounds like** (same "complex project" question):
  - **L3:** a feature-level bet in an existing product (name a user, write the strategy).
  - **L4:** owning a problem end to end with cross-functional dependencies; a failure and
    recovery arc.
  - **L5:** the team's whole roadmap; pushing back on exec asks with data so leadership de-scopes
    them *themselves*.
  - **L6:** building an org-level capability across partnerships, engineering, and community, with
    principled sequencing ("the first cohort is a cultural statement").
  - **L7:** a stalled cross-org problem nobody asked you to take, which you identified as the most
    important thing the company wasn't doing.
- **Senior+ mistakes:**
  - Story complexity not calibrated to the level. A Staff candidate with no cross-org or
    self-identified stories has a problem no other prep fixes.
  - Not confirming the role's level early. Ask the recruiter, and ask to interview at your
    target level.
  - **Leading with your weakest story.** The first answer anchors the calibration, so lead with
    your highest-complexity story.
  - Influence described in the abstract.
  - Signaling a weak position (never share a downleveled competing offer).
- **Senior+ green flags:**
  - State your level in the intro.
  - Show GM instincts: marketing, GTM, org dynamics, "this wins or fails because of me."
  - **End on philosophy, not a metric.**
  - Treat it as a work conversation, with take-it-or-leave-it energy.

## Building the bank: about 10 stories
- **5 flagship projects:** the most impressive work, with real decisions and outcomes. Spread them
  across types (0→1, optimization, turnaround, data-driven pivot, cross-functional).
- **5 people and leadership stories:**
  - Conflict with someone *above* you.
  - Conflict with a peer or cross-functional partner.
  - A failure. Senior and above: a high-stakes miss with real consequences, because a bigger
    failure is more credible.
  - Mentoring or developing others (core for senior and above).
  - Negative feedback that changed you (the specific feedback, your honest reaction, the concrete
    behavior change).
- **Mine your archive:** every resume version, a LinkedIn PDF, promo packets, self-reviews, brag
  docs, 360s, and specs. Raw and unedited is best.
- **Audit the level:** each level up means a wider circle of impact. Thin stories need digging or
  replacing.
- **Excavate with an interviewer** (AI or a friend): the impact; the key decision; the approach and
  the rejected alternative; the tradeoffs; who you influenced; the moments of tension; how you got
  alignment; what process or strategy changed; what you'd do differently.
- **Tag each story with 3 to 5 of these 15:** stakeholder conflict · data-driven decision · heavy
  cross-functional work · high complexity · tradeoffs under constraint · ambiguity · influence
  without authority · 0→1 · unassigned ownership · being wrong or failing · customer obsession ·
  multiple orgs · difficult feedback · time pressure or cut scope · mentorship. Ten stories × 3 to
  5 tags = 30 to 50 matches. **In the room, you match tags. You don't hunt for "the right story."**
- **Adapt per company** as a separate step: research the company and write an "ideal candidate
  profile," then pick the 5 or 6 best-matching stories and the tags to emphasize.

## Practicing: anchors, not scripts
- **Why scripts fail:** recitation sounds flat; labs push three or four levels deep; and STAR
  answers polished with AI sound generic. *What wins now is deep prep that still sounds unscripted.*
  (Amazon still wants STAR.)
- **Opening:** the bottom line up front, in one or two sentences covering the project, the result,
  and the impact. No context preamble. "I rebuilt onboarding and cut churn 18% in a quarter."
- **Closing:** *what changed because of you*, which is bigger than a result: what the company did
  next, plus what you learned. Below senior, show growth. Senior and above, show org or strategic
  impact (it became a model, shifted a process, unlocked investment, changed how you lead).
- **Three must-include scenes,** in any order:
  - **Stakes:** why it mattered and how hard it was ("the CEO raised churn at the all-hands; we
    had 30 days").
  - **The key decision:** what you chose, the alternative you rejected, the tradeoff. Decisions,
    not activities. If there was no alternative, it wasn't a decision.
  - **The biggest challenge to your approach:** resistance, complexity, the moment it nearly fell
    apart.
- **Flex the middle by question:** influence leans on the decision and challenge; tradeoffs lean
  on the decision; pressure leans on stakes and challenge. The opening and closing stay fixed.
- **Five checks before a story is ready:**
  - Does the opening hook? Try it on a non-PM.
  - Does something change over time? Otherwise it's a status update.
  - Is it about *you*? Aim for about 80% of the words. "We launched" makes you invisible.
  - Would it hold attention at a dinner party?
  - Is it the right scope for your level?
- **The 10 follow-ups to drill:** impact? What did *you* do? Why that approach? Why not the
  alternative? Tradeoffs? What if X failed? How did you measure success? How did you know you were
  right? Who were the stakeholders, and how did you work with them? What would you do differently?
- **Drill loop:** tell it aloud, take 2 to 5 minutes of hard follow-ups, then flag vague,
  unsupported, or under-scoped moments and make one change. **Wherever you stumble is a hole in
  the story. Dig up the real detail; don't patch the wording.** Then the retell test: close
  everything and tell it from scratch.

## Rubric: all L&D questions (5 dimensions, Strong No Hire → Strong Hire)
**Passion is the biggest differentiator now.**
1. **Scope of impact:** make the scope *visible* through detail. A one-line company-wide effort
   reads like a feature fix. Senior: problems you identified. If every story starts "my manager
   asked me to…," you're signaling one level down.
2. **Stakeholder management** (separate from scope). Strong Hire: names the person (role, level,
   function), what they were protecting, the approach you chose and why not another, and how the
   resolution connected to the outcome. Strong No Hire: no stakeholders, or "everyone agreed."
3. **Ownership:** did you wait, or move? Did you stay when it got hard or political? The twist
   question, *"you pushed back but later realized you were wrong,"* is an **ownership** question:
   own mistakes as directly as wins. Strong Hire: explicitly not your job, or stuck under another
   team, and you drove it to an outcome knowing the risk to your time, relationships, and
   credibility. At Staff and above (Anthropic, OpenAI, Stripe): problems you *surfaced*, i.e.,
   *will you tell us what we need to hear, or only what we asked for?*
4. **Communication:** a story with a shape. A hook, stakes you can follow, a decision with an
   alternative, a close about change. Strong Hire: real tension (speed vs. quality, stakeholder
   vs. user) and a transferable insight the interviewer learns from.
5. **Passion:** *texture*, not enthusiasm. A calm candidate can score Strong Hire. Spontaneous
   detail, genuine uncertainty, a reflection too specific to script, a story *only you could
   tell*. The interviewer remembers it after the loop.

## Traps
- Recited scripts.
- A list of activities with no decision.
- "We" instead of "I."
- Abstract influence.
- Ending on a bare metric.
- Under-scoped stories for the level.
- Opening with your weakest story.
- A failure story with nothing at stake.
